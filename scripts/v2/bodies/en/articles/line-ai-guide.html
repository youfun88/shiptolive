What are the three kinds of LINE AI chatbot, and who is each for?

Most LINE messages to a business ask the same few things: opening hours, prices, how to book, where to park. There are three ways to answer them automatically.

1. LINE's keyword auto-replies

A standard LINE Official Account feature. You set keywords and fixed answers; when a message matches a keyword, LINE sends that answer. It costs nothing extra, but the customer has to type your keyword. A different wording, or two questions in one message, may get no reply.

Fits: a handful of fixed answers, such as "address" or "hours".

2. LINE's built-in "AI chatbot (beta)"

A beta feature LINE launched in Taiwan in November 2025. You register FAQs in the account manager; when a customer asks, the AI replies with the FAQ that matches best, even when the wording differs. According to LINE Biz, it needs the Chat Pro option (an NT$100 subscription), has a monthly reply limit, and is recommended for accounts with fewer than 25,000 friends. Accounts in medical and welfare, finance, religion, politics and government, and law may not be able to use it even with the subscription.

Fits: a small shop with a few dozen fixed questions that wants to set it up itself and isn't in one of those industries.

3. Third-party AI assistants and setup services

These connect your Official Account to an outside AI system through LINE's Messaging API. They vary a lot, but fall into three groups:

  • Omnichannel inbox and marketing platforms: LINE, Facebook, Instagram and other chats in one inbox, plus broadcasts, tags and CRM; AI replies are one feature. You usually set them up yourself, and pricing is typically by seats, contacts or AI credits.
  • Custom projects: a vendor builds an AI assistant around your systems (CRM, orders). Usually quoted per project, for larger companies.
  • Done-for-you setup and upkeep: the provider writes the Q&A with you, connects the AI, tests it and tunes it every month. You keep replying in the LINE Official Account app as before. Ship to Live works this way.

Fits: many questions, answers drawn from your website and files, hand-off alerts, or an industry where LINE's built-in AI may not be available.

What is a "monthly active contact"?

The number of different customers who messaged your account this month (Taipei calendar month).

Example: Ms. Wang asks about your hours, changes her booking twice and asks about parking: 10 messages, but 1 active contact. If 299 other customers also message you that month, the total is 300, exactly what the Light plan includes.

The three common billing models:

  • By messages or AI credits: each AI reply uses credits, so a chatty month costs more and is harder to predict.
  • By seats: by how many staff log in. Fits companies with a support team that assigns chats.
  • By monthly active contacts: by how many different customers wrote that month. Extra messages from the same customer cost nothing, so it's easier to budget.

Whatever the model, ask: does the AI stop answering when you reach the limit? Ours keeps answering, and the overage never costs more than the next plan up.

10 questions to ask any LINE AI chatbot vendor

  1. Does the AI answer only from my data? When it doesn't know, does it say so and hand off, or guess?
  2. Which source wins when they disagree? For example, a price on the website that differs from the Q&A.
  3. How does hand-off work? What triggers it (quotes, complaints, "I want a person"), what does the customer see, and how am I told?
  4. Whose name is the account in? The LINE Official Account and the Messaging API provider should be in your company's name.
  5. Do you need my password? You shouldn't. LINE's Manage permissions screen lets you invite an admin and remove them later.
  6. How is it billed? Per message, per seat or per active contact? Does the AI stop at the limit? What is the most I can pay in a month?
  7. What does setup include? Who writes the Q&A, and do we test it together with real questions before launch?
  8. Can it read live data? Is stock, availability or listing status read from a sheet or system, or typed into the Q&A? Can the AI place orders or take payments?
  9. Do you know my industry's rules? Ask how they limit what the AI answers for medical, legal, finance, education or real estate.
  10. Who tunes it after launch? How often do you read real chats and fix weak answers? How long is the contract?

How to set it up: 6 steps

This is the order we follow; it applies to most providers.

  1. List what to prepare. Common questions and standard answers, services and prices, hours and address (days off, directions, parking), how to book or buy, returns and cancellations, cases that must go to a person, brand tone, and things the AI must not say. Your website, menu and price list are a good start.
  2. Grant access, not your password. In the LINE Official Account manager, go to Settings, then Manage permissions, create an invite link and add the provider as an admin. We use that access to turn on the Messaging API, connect the AI and set up the provider in your company's name. You can remove us any time.
  3. Draft the Q&A from your website. We build a first version from your website, menu, price list or past LINE chats. You confirm each answer and mark the ones that go to a person, such as bulk-order quotes or complaints.
  4. Test with real questions. Use questions customers actually asked, plus rewordings, two questions in one message, English, "I want a person", and things that aren't in your data. Nothing goes live until you've seen and approved the answers.
  5. Go live. Check old keyword auto-replies so they don't answer alongside the AI, and confirm hand-off alerts reach you. You can still reply yourself any time in the LINE Official Account app or at chat.line.biz.
  6. Tune it every month. Read real chats and add what it answered poorly or handed off often; post temporary changes (closing early, sold out) as a notice; check the month's usage.

Industry rules to keep in mind (Taiwan)

An AI assistant only says faster what you would say anyway, so your industry's rules still apply. This is how we set it up; for your own case, ask your legal adviser. Statutes link to Taiwan's Laws & Regulations Database.

  • Clinics, dentists, aesthetic clinics: the AI never diagnoses or suggests medication; under Article 11 of the Physicians Act a doctor must examine a patient in person before treating or prescribing. Medical advertising is limited by Articles 85–86 of the Medical Care Act, so the AI doesn't volunteer promotions or treatment claims. Health data is sensitive under Article 6 of the Personal Data Protection Act, so it doesn't ask about symptoms.
  • Law, accounting and insurance offices: the AI answers office information only (booking, published fees, documents to bring, deadlines). Case questions go to the lawyer or accountant; Article 127 of the Attorney Regulation Act penalizes non-lawyers who handle litigation for profit. For finance, the FSC's AI guidelines say AI that talks to consumers should be disclosed.
  • Cram schools and classes: seats and timetables come from a live sheet, and closures (typhoon days) from a notice. Article 9 of the Supplementary and Continuing Education Act bars false enrolment advertising, so the AI never promises grades.
  • Restaurants and bakeries: menu files and a daily notice. Allergen labeling rules (Food Safety and Sanitation Act, Article 22, and MOHW's allergen rules) mainly cover packaged food, but a wrong answer can hurt someone: the AI repeats what the menu says and asks the customer to confirm with staff.
  • Real estate agents: Article 21 of the Real Estate Broking Management Act requires ads and sales information to match the facts. Listing status and prices should come from a live listing sheet, not a stale Q&A. Viewings, offers and loans go to the agent.
